Segment One

Policy context

The legal floor, the district choices it leaves open, and the limits of compliance on its own.

9:00 – 9:20 · 20 minutes

Public Act 104-0657 · the definition

“Wireless communication device” does not include a device issued by the school or a teacher for educational purposes.

SB 2427 · signed July 28, 2026 · effective 2027–28 school year

What the law requires

Bell to bell, with a high school exception

Kindergarten through grade 8

Restricted from the first arrival bell to the final dismissal bell — including lunch, passing periods, and other non-instructional time.

High school

Bell-to-bell as the default, with a district opt-out permitting access during passing periods and lunch. Use during class remains prohibited either way.

  • Effective for the 2027–28 school year. ISBE released its template policy September 1, 2026.
  • Districts have roughly eighteen months to adopt, communicate, and prepare.

What the statute covers

A broad list, and one large exemption

Regulated

Cellphones. Smartwatches and smart glasses. Gaming devices. Personal laptops and tablets. Wearables generally.

Exempt by definition

Any device the school or a teacher issues for educational purposes. Your entire one-to-one fleet sits outside the statute.

This is not a loophole and not an oversight. It is the correct scope for a distraction statute. It does mean the law settles one channel and leaves four others entirely to you.

Mandated exceptions

The law already concedes that a blanket rule blocks somebody

  • Medical need — devices required to monitor or manage a health condition.
  • IEPs and 504 plans — where a device is part of the student’s special education services.
  • English learners — where a device provides language translation.
  • Emergencies, and case-by-case determinations for students who are caregivers for family at home.
The question underneath

Every one of these is an access decision made in advance. The audit asks which access decisions you have not yet made in advance.

Segment Two

The friction framework

Four dimensions, and one distinction that does most of the work: difficulty that builds capacity against difficulty that blocks participation.

9:20 – 9:50 · 30 minutes

The distinction

Not all difficulty is the same kind of difficulty

Productive friction

Difficulty that builds capacity: the generative cognitive work, real dialogue, authorship, and accountability through which understanding forms.

Drafting a claim by hand before opening a tool.

Exclusionary friction

Difficulty that blocks access or participation without contributing to learning — often by treating one cognitive profile as the unmarked norm.

Requiring written-only formats that shut out multilingual learners.

A policy decision can produce either one. Usually it produces some of both, and nobody has named which is which.

Four dimensions

Where the work has to happen

The head

Noetic

The synthesis, argument, and meaning-making that must happen inside the learner. Lost when cognitive labor moves from the student to a generating system.

The room

Rhetorical

Authentic dialogue and genuine exchange. Simulated dialogue rarely resists, disagrees, or holds anyone to account.

The world

Existential

Claims tied to claimants. Text can now be produced with no consciousness standing behind it; authorship and intellectual risk are what resist that.

The system

Infrastructural

Policy, assessment, leadership, professional learning, and governance — the conditions that let the other three be sustained. This is your dimension.

Segment Two activity

Sort the friction

Card sortClassify each one, then check the reading.12 min

01Students draft a claim by hand before opening any AI tool.

Productive, noetic. The work of forming a position happens in the learner. The difficulty is the point, and it is doing something.

02All work must be typed and submitted in writing. No oral or recorded options.

Exclusionary. It treats one output mode as the unmarked norm and blocks multilingual learners and students with writing-related disabilities without adding any learning the task required.

03Phones are collected at the door. A student who helps manage a parent’s medical care cannot check messages.

Exclusionary as written — and the statute already anticipates it through case-by-case caregiver determinations. The failure is not the rule. It is designing the exception after the rule instead of alongside it.

04Students explain, in two sentences, one revision they made and why the evidence warranted it.

Productive, existential and rhetorical. It ties a claim to a claimant. It also costs almost nothing to implement, which matters when you are asking teachers to absorb a change.

05The approved AI platform opens with a suggested thesis before the student has written anything.

Neither — this is friction removed. A vendor default decided what the task requires, and no one in the district chose it. Infrastructural conditions overrode instructional intent silently. This is the case the audit studio exists to catch.

06Every essay must be submitted with a full revision history and version log.

Drifts exclusionary. Revision histories are clues a teacher reads, not proof of understanding. A district that starts grading the documentation has built a compliance exercise with a research vocabulary — and it lands hardest on students who work in ways the log does not capture.

Segment Three

Cases and evidence

What the research actually licenses you to say, and five implementation questions the statute does not settle.

9:50 – 10:20 · 30 minutes

Four findings

The evidence supports acting — with more precision than the debate allows

Beland & Murphy, 2016

Found: exam performance gains after English schools banned phones, concentrated among lower-achieving students.

Limits: a different country, an earlier technology, and a policy environment that predates generative tools.

SMART Schools, 2025

Found: across 30 English secondary schools and 1,227 adolescents, no evidence of better wellbeing and no evidence of lower overall phone use.

Limits: cross-sectional. It compared schools that already differed, so it cannot tell you what would happen if your school changed.

Dutch national evaluation

Found: schools reported improved concentration and climate after restrictions.

Limits: reported experience, not measured outcomes. Both can be true at once, which points to a measurement gap rather than a contradiction.

Pew Research Center, 2024

Found: 72 percent of U.S. public high school teachers call cellphone distraction a major classroom problem.

Limits: it establishes that the problem is real. It says nothing about what any given remedy produces.

Forty years of research

Does the device matter, or what you do with it?

Tamim and colleagues pooled 25 meta-analyses covering 1,055 primary studies to ask whether classroom technology affects achievement. The overall mean effect was 0.35. Their moderator analysis splits it in two:

Supporting instruction

ES = 0.42  (k = 10)
Technology that supports students in constructing understanding.

Direct instruction

ES = 0.31  (k = 15)
Technology that delivers content or drills it.

Read it carefully. Both are moderate positive effects. Technology used for direct instruction is not inert, and anyone who tells you the research says otherwise is overstating it. What the margin supports is narrower and more useful: how a task uses the device matters more than whether the device is in the room.

The limit: this corpus predates smartphones and generative AI entirely. It tells you something about instructional design. It tells you nothing about phone policy.

Segment Three activity

Defensible, or one step too far?

Claims ladderFour sentences a board member might hear. Which ones survive scrutiny?8 min

01“Research shows that banning phones raises test scores.”

Overclaimed. Beland and Murphy found gains in English schools before generative AI, concentrated in one group of students. Stated this way it becomes a universal prediction the study never made.

02“A restriction may particularly benefit students who are currently struggling.”

Defensible. This is close to what Beland and Murphy actually support, and the hedge is doing honest work rather than covering you.

03“Studies show phone policies don’t improve student wellbeing.”

Overclaimed. SMART Schools found no association in a cross-sectional comparison of schools that already differed. Absence of an association is not evidence that changing a policy has no effect.

04“Teachers report phone distraction as a serious classroom problem.”

Defensible. Pew, 2024. Note what it licenses: acting on a real problem. Not a prediction about what the action will produce.

Five implementation questions

What the template will not decide for you

Storage

Pouches, classroom caddies, lockers, or backpacks. Each carries a different cost, a different liability question, and a different daily time tax on teachers.

Enforcement

Who confiscates, who stores, who returns, and what the escalation ladder is. Uneven enforcement produces discipline disparities faster than almost anything else.

Exceptions

Who determines a case-by-case caregiver exception, on what timeline, and how a student requests one without announcing their circumstances to a class.

One-to-one devices

Exempt from the statute, present in every classroom. If the concern was attention, the device you issued is still there.

Generative AI

What your approved platforms do the moment a student opens them — and whether that behavior matches what your teachers were told to expect.

How the studio works

Three questions, five channels

  1. What does our policy actually say about this channel? Quote it if you can. “Nothing” is a legitimate and common answer.
  2. What is the gap? The distance between what the policy says and what happens in a classroom on a Tuesday.
  3. Who owns it, and by when? A name and a date. Not a committee, not “curriculum.”
Ground rule

You will not finish all five well. Do two properly rather than five badly. The instrument comes home with you.

Segment Five

System alignment

Connecting the audit to pedagogy, equity, family communication, and the conditions that decide whether any of it survives contact with a school year.

11:20 – 11:45 · 25 minutes

What fills the space

A restriction creates room. It does not decide what goes in it.

One assessment, rebuilt in three moves — a middle-school history team asking students to explain why two accounts of the same event differ:

  1. Students meet two short contrasting sources, name the disagreement, and offer a provisional explanation. This is the first moment a teacher can see how a student reads a source.
  2. Students meet a competing explanation. A classmate can supply it. A prepared counterargument can supply it. An approved tool can supply it — if someone has checked that the tool will challenge a reading rather than hand over a better one.
  3. Students explain one revision they made and why the evidence warranted it. Two or three sentences. A recording works. A short conference works.
Notice

Someone has to find the source pairs, produce accessible versions, confirm what the platform does, and tell the team that a shorter, better-evidenced explanation will not read as a drop in rigor at evaluation time. None of that is a teacher decision.

RegisterAdd a row per risk. Saved in your browser.10 min

The conditions

Four things only leadership can grant

Time

A teaching team needs several hours together to rebuild one assessment well. Those hours exist inside a year that is already full. Name what comes off the calendar.

Access

Ask which supports students need to participate in the chosen task. A student who listens to a text may still perform the whole comparison. A student handed a generated interpretation does not.

Platform

Someone in the technology office confirms what the approved tool does by default, rather than leaving each teacher to discover it alone in October.

Evaluation

Tell principals what the redesigned work looks like from the doorway. A class arguing about two documents does not photograph like rigor, and teachers read that mismatch fast.

A district that adds the redesign without subtracting anything has decided the redesign is optional. Everyone in the building will understand that decision correctly.

Family communication

The message that arrives before the policy does

  • Parents hear “my child cannot reach me” before they hear anything about attention or learning. Answer that first, in the first paragraph.
  • Say plainly how a family reaches a student in an emergency, and how a student requests an exception without disclosing their circumstances to a classroom.
  • Do not promise outcomes the evidence will not carry. A letter that claims test scores will rise is a letter you will be asked about in eighteen months.

Segment Six activity

Five commitments that fit in one motion

Before you claim anything

Three questions, kept apart

Implementation

Is the restriction workable?

Staff experience, interruptions, enforcement time, and the pattern of exceptions. Confiscations falling can mean compliance. It can also mean uneven enforcement, or a change in how staff report.

Access

Can students participate?

Whether the supports students need survived the new conditions. Describe the problems without turning any student’s accommodation into an anecdote.

Learning

What can students explain or do?

A reasoning checkpoint gives a teacher something to interpret. It does not certify authorship. Whether a capacity transfers requires evidence from later work with different sources.

Districts collapse these three into one and then draw conclusions none of the evidence supports. One redesigned assessment cannot show that a phone policy caused an instructional outcome.
